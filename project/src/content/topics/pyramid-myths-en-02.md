## Introduction

Many people carry a picture of “the whole world shining at once”: pyramids finished while the Yellow Emperor, Yao, Shun, and Yu walk the third millennium BCE. Archaeology and classical chronicles are not on the same frequency. This installment uses textual chronology and archaeological dates on two tracks for Egypt and China—not to rank greatness, but to see multiple centers out of sync.

## At a Glance

- Giza’s pyramids rose roughly in the 2600s BCE; North China at the same time held many prehistoric cultures; verifiable Shang history comes much later.
- Classics placing ancient kings “contemporary with pyramids” read smoothly; archaeological dates often do not—keep the two timelines separate.
- Not who is greater, but that Egypt’s stone mountains and China’s later bronze, writing, and underground royal tombs are different paths.
- Masonry, aliens, and conspiracy theories appear in other installments.

## Roughly When Was Giza?

**When were the three Giza pyramids (Khufu, Khafre, Menkaure) built?**

- **Petrie**: Fourth Dynasty, c. 4000 to 3800 BCE
- **Burr**: All Egyptian pyramids c. 4000 to 2500 BCE
- **Mainstream archaeology today**: Khufu c. 2580 to 2560 BCE; Khafre and Menkaure slightly later

Petrie (1853–1942) and Burr’s dates run early; using them directly against China lengthens the feeling of “Egypt far ahead”—so this installment takes c. 2600 to 2500 BCE as the Giza peak, closer to modern Egyptology and within the key Chinese prehistoric window Eberhard discusses.

## In the Same Window, Where Was China?

Eberhard (1907–1989, German sinologist, *A History of China*) held that archaeology in the third millennium BCE shows no highly developed civilization; a fusion we can call “Chinese civilization” formed only after about 1300 BCE—this archaeological axis pulls directly away from the literary axis that puts the Yellow Emperor, Yao, Shun, and Yu in the same era as the pyramids.

**Same time, two places (China after Eberhard)**

**c. 2600 to 2500 BCE**

- Egypt: Fourth Dynasty; tomb pyramids built with labor on the order of one hundred thousand
- China: No unified state; many prehistoric cultures on the Yellow and Yangtze rivers

**c. 2000 to 1600 BCE**

- Egypt: Middle Kingdom and others
- China: Possible Xia traces; farming, some bronze, no writing yet

**From c. 1600 BCE**

- Egypt: Successive dynasties
- China: Shang: Anyang, oracle bone script, bronze ritual vessels, royal tombs

When Giza was built, mainland China had no comparable dynasty, literate state, or giant stone engineering. Eberhard (as above) treated pre–Warring States compiled names of ancient kings as later projections back onto the third millennium—reading pyramid dates is good practice for not forcing literary narrative and archaeological dates into one picture.

## Many Centers, Out of Sync

Burr and Rawlinson (nineteenth-century Egyptian studies) recorded on the order of seven million tons of stone, granite shipped five hundred miles by river to Giza; North China at the same time had regional cultures side by side—not “who lagged,” but peaks at different times and material conditions, without using pyramids as the only ruler.

## Display Engineering Took Different Roads

Rawlinson (1812–1902) noted Egypt’s flat plain, pushing people toward giant stone that would shock posterity. Eberhard (1907–1989) described early Chinese farming settlements, earthen walled towns, underground royal tombs—the Shang “still had no large mound tombs.” Egypt poured state power into visible stone mountains; China later poured elite resources into bronze ritual, writing, and lineage politics. Eberhard (as above) also discussed scarce copper and tin, rich loess—material conditions explain why China could not copy Egypt’s stone path, not a one-line “ranking of civilizations.”

## “Same Era” Does Not Mean “Able to Dialogue”

China opposite Giza was on the eve of Longshan culture, many cultural circles—no pharaoh, no unified corvée. Only by the Shang (c. 1600 BCE) do kingship, writing, bronze, and human-sacrifice royal tombs offer comparable elements; by then Egypt’s Old Kingdom pyramid age had passed. The two timelines miss each other in history; they are not one civilization race.

Traditional chronologies place the Yellow Emperor, Yao, Shun, and Yu in the third millennium alongside pyramids, easily creating a false sense of synchrony. Eberhard (1907–1989) stressed that names of kings before c. 2200 BCE first appear in texts only around 400 BCE—this installment uses the Egyptian case to remind readers to separate literary and archaeological layers when reading early China too.

## Craft and Later Megaproject Impulses

Petrie (1853–1942) showed Egyptian stonework at a very high level (sawing, tube drilling, leveling). Eberhard (1907–1989) said Shang bronze casting was “rarely surpassed afterward”; royal tombs were underground cruciform chambers—different craft types, not a simple higher/lower comparison.

Giles (1845–1935, British sinologist, *China and the Chinese*) more often compared China with Greece on custom than with Egypt—modern scholars too saw ritual–writing–agrarian empire and Nile stone–divine tomb as different lineages.

China later still had the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, Han and Tang mausoleums, the Great Wall, the Grand Canal—state mobilization on both sides—but mostly earth, wood, brick, terracotta figures, and underground palaces, rarely ground-level stone cones. Centralization and superscale labor exist across cultures; materials, who dies for whom, and how memory is kept differ with geography and belief. That echoes this installment: parallel systems, not a single ranking.

## Summary

Seeing pyramids and China as “contemporary” teaches less who was earlier or greater than how humans along different rivers and resources each explored “state—memory—eternity.” Egypt raised stone monuments for pharaohs; China in the following millennium used bronze and writing to set models for kings—adjacent in time, separate in path.

## Worth Rethinking

How much of your “five thousand years” timeline comes from classics, and how much from archaeology? Can you draw two axes without forcing them into one image?
