Have you ever woken up with only a few fragments of a dream left?

A house you don't recognize. Someone you haven't seen in years. A door that won't open no matter what you do. You can't remember what came first, yet the moment you tell someone about it, it becomes a story with a beginning and an end.

You are not lying. Your mind simply cannot bear a pile of unconnected pieces.

## 1. The Dream Patches Its Own Gaps

Freud wrote about a detail few people notice. Once a dream is made, there is one last step: the scattered fragments are stitched together so the whole thing seems to make sense.

> But it's only a dream.

He said this sentence often turns up inside the dream itself. Its job is to make what just happened seem small, so the dream can go on and the sleeper can keep sleeping.

What startled me most was not that dreams deceive us. It was when this reassurance appears. It always comes at the very moment something feels most wrong.

So the next time you hear "it's fine" in a dream, it probably doesn't mean you've woken up. It means you want to sleep a little longer.

## 2. One Line Stitches Several Reports Together

Last week there was a headline: "Energy ceasefire takes effect immediately."

That same week, the other side said its energy facilities were still under attack, and Russia gave no public confirmation. Then came word of a deal to buy diesel, and senators from both parties signed a letter opposing it.

I no longer want to argue about who is right. I just recognize a very familiar move: several contradictory reports, stitched into one storyline by a single sentence.

"Takes effect immediately" works like "it's only a dream" in the dream. It may not be false. But once you hear it, whatever is still happening seems to matter a little less. Just as a person who wakes joins the scattered pieces of a dream into one complete story, the parts that don't fit are quietly smoothed away.

## 3. The Ceasefire Is Most Stable in the Headline

Looking back on that week, where did the ceasefire hold up best?

Ukraine said its energy facilities were still being hit. Russia gave no public confirmation. The only thing that stood firm, unchanged for the whole week, was that one line in the headline.

The same week, there was news of an arrangement for the United States to buy diesel from Russia. Senators from both parties wrote in protest, calling it "a gift to an adversary and a betrayal of allies." One hand says the energy war must stop; the other does energy business with the side that started the war. Even a dream would struggle to patch together a plot like that.

As for the people who still worry about blackouts at night, they would find it hard to believe the ceasefire has taken effect.

---

When we wake, we love to turn the dream into a story.

The dream does this because the mind cannot stand a pile of fragments. The news does it too. One line, "takes effect immediately," joins the attacks still going on, the side that hasn't responded, and the deal struck at the same time into one storyline that sounds as if it makes sense.

But the smoother the patch, the further it often drifts from what really happened.

Being awake doesn't mean refusing to fill in the story. It means that after you've filled it in, you still remember the pieces that were smoothed away.
