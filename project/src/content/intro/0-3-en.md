## Introduction

The first two pieces said: reading stores **factual basis**; writing for an **observation point** shows whether things connect; **composing** stays with you, **drafting** can go to AI for polish. Many still stall here—not no ideas, but not knowing what to do **after** you have one: call AI to format? Or flip books for a shiny quote to paste?

Between those sits a process I call three steps of **composing**: **evidence → digest → view**. It's the middle of the writing pyramid: reading at the base, drafting at the top; **skip these three and the top layer is only an empty shell.**

## At a Glance

- **Observation point**: the question you're standing on now and what you need to clarify (stance, not yet conclusion).
- **Factual basis**: passages saved from reading, ready to hold up the observation point.
- **Evidence**: factual basis that stands on the page—clear source, retellable in your words.
- **Digest**: wiring factual basis to your observation point—reorder, compare, drop what doesn't serve.
- **View**: a judgment on the observation point open to correction—not a quote, a claim you'll own.
- **In one line**: evidence anchors, digest gives flesh, view is your face.

## Evidence: Make Factual Basis Stand First

Evidence is not "find a line that sounds impressive." Minimum: **who said it, where**; further, can you **retell it correctly without the original**?

**Charlotte M. Mason** (British educator, 1842–1923), in *An Essay Towards a Philosophy of Education*, treats **narration** as the post-read test: read a passage, retell in your own words; keep asking "What next?"—plot and argument string together. She quotes an old saying: the mind can only know what it can **produce after questioning itself**. That's evidence in practice: **not labeling, but running material through your own Q&A chain.**

In practice I keep three lines per factual basis I will use: author and title, the bit that matters for the observation point, how I retell it. If I can't retell it, evidence isn't done—at best I've "seen" it.

## Digest: Turn Others' Material into Your Argument

With evidence done, material may still be shaped like someone else's. **Digest** is wiring factual basis to the observation point: what to keep, contrast, or discard.

**Ralph Waldo Emerson** (American essayist and thinker, 1803–1882), in "The American Scholar," contrasts **Man Thinking** with the bookworm: books record past minds; great works take the world in, rearrange it within the author, and **speak**—in comes fact, out comes thought. Digest is that rearrangement: not hoarding another volume, but reordering for **your observation point**.

**William Henry Burnham** (American psychologist, 1855–1919), in *An Historical Sketch of the Conceptions of Memory among the Ancients*, traces views of memory: before writing spread, knowledge lived in memory preserved and passed on; ancients prized **retrievable, retellable** memory over passive storage. Highlights and bookmarks that never enter retelling stay warehouse, not digest.

A common digest failure: five book summaries side by side with no answer to what they **support, oppose, or revise** about the observation point. I force one sentence: "So for this observation point, this factual basis means…"—if I can't write it, digest hasn't started.

## View: Judgment on the Observation Point Finishes "Composing"

A view isn't three words of "I feel," nor the observation point rephrased. **Bertrand Russell** (British philosopher, 1872–1970), at the opening of *Philosophy*, says everyday "knowledge" is overconfident and vague; we need **judgment open to correction**. A view is that settlement: **what you claim and don't claim about the observation point, and where the boundary is**—new evidence arrives, you can revise.

Evidence gives anchors to check back; digest gives discipline to choose; the view is the line you'll sign and rewrite.

## The Middle of the Writing Pyramid

| Layer | What you do |
|----|----------|
| Base · Read | Input factual basis, highlight, tag "might connect to some observation point" |
| **Middle · Compose** | **Evidence → digest → view** |
| Top · Draft | Hand what's already thought to AI for layout and polish |

## Conclusion

After you have an idea, don't rush to "write beautifully." First **evidence** (factual basis stands), then **digest** (reorder for the observation point), then **view** (judgment open to correction on the observation point). All three are **composing**; **drafting** can go to AI. With this middle layer clear, books don't stay on the shelf—they become judgments you'll stand behind in public.
